# Chapter 7

The return to Westfall took six days.

Without Aldren's pace they moved faster, but Sehir's wounds and the treacherous valley roads took most of that gain back. Twice they hid from prowling beasts at dusk. Once they crossed a flood-cut span by crawling along a fallen pine while river water tore white around the trunk below.

Arienne never tried to run.

She tested the contract in subtler ways: delayed answers, careful omissions, suggestive phrasing.

Each time, the mark at her throat tightened and forced correction before she could push too far.

By the time Westfall's towers rose above the sea cliffs, both of them looked like they had survived a campaign.

They were welcomed warmly.

Commander Halvren came down to the harbor stair with a reception detail, not out of sentiment but protocol. Advisor Sehir was the crown's senior justice authority in the region, with oversight over prison budget, supply approvals, and emergency transfer orders.

"Advisor," she said, bowing. "Westfall welcomes you back."

Sehir returned the gesture. "Commander."

Warden Corvin stood at her shoulder, key ring heavy at his belt.

"Quarters are prepared, and records are ready if your inspection has expanded," he said.

Sehir gave them the public version first.

"Blackstone's upper galleries failed during assessment. Professor Aldren was killed in the collapse. Lady Arienne survived and remains under my direct authority as expedition mage for reconstruction operations."

No names.

No mention of the queen.

No confession spoken where rumor could outrun strategy. And no hint that Arienne had once been sent to kill him. That truth stayed locked behind his teeth, where it might one day return to the capital as a blade turned inward.

Halvren's expression tightened at Aldren's death, but she did not challenge the chain of authority.

"Then we adapt," she said.

Sehir turned to Corvin. "I need prison ledgers, violent repeat rosters, and current food burden totals. Immediately."

"At once," Corvin said.

Within the hour, Sehir was in the keep records room with stacks of ledgers and a valley transit map spread beneath brass weights. Arienne stood nearby in a plain cloak, presented as the expedition's surviving arcane specialist and officially listed as court-appointed mage oversight for penal field labor.

It was a perfect cover because it was ordinary.

Forced labor transfers to remote reconstruction sites had precedent. Court mages supervising high-risk details had precedent. Emergency justice orders after mass casualty events had precedent.

Sehir built everything inside those precedents.

By evening, sealed directives were in motion:

- Reopen Westfall-to-Blackstone transfer chain under emergency reconstruction authority.
- Limit candidates to violent repeat offenders and chronic disciplinary failures.
- Apply indentured restitution terms with sentence-weight reduction for compliance.
- Keep legal custody with Westfall records while field command runs through court authority.

Halvren accepted the logistics.

Corvin accepted the pressure because it reduced isolation load and freed guard rotations.

Neither man nor woman knew what actually enforced compliance.

Contracts were executed in private review cells under the guise of sentence attestation.

No witnesses beyond Sehir and the inmate. Arienne remained outside each door as visible security and silent warning.

Inside, Sehir weighed petitions, set terms, and sealed enforcement with controlled flashes of white light hidden under collars and cuffs whenever possible.

From the outside, the results looked procedural.

From the inside, they were absolute.

Men who had lived by violence emerged tight-jawed, shaken, and suddenly cooperative. Their hatred did not vanish overnight. It simply stopped driving their actions.

By the tenth binding, transfer discipline exceeded anything Corvin had achieved in years.

By the fifteenth, Sehir could feel the thread to the goddess warming at the edge of sleep.

Not restoration.

Recovery.

On the second night, he found the ledger fractures.

At a glance, Corvin's records were immaculate: ration tallies, infirmary notes, disciplinary logs, repair requisitions. At a closer read, the seams split. Isolation deaths marked as fever lined up with missing meal entries. Use-of-force reports lacked witness signatures. Drainage repairs had been funded twice and completed once.

Neglect at best.

Abuse hidden in paperwork at worst.

Sehir summoned Corvin after midnight.

The warden arrived alone and wary. Sehir laid the pages in a line across the table.

"Explain these."

Corvin read in silence, jaw working.

"This fortress runs on shortage," he said at last. "Shortage distorts policy."

"Shortage does not authorize hidden deaths," Sehir replied.

Corvin's stare hardened. "You govern from parchment. I govern from stone. If discipline slips, people die."

"Then record what you do," Sehir said. "Do not falsify it."

Corvin did not deny the falsification.

That was enough.

Sehir stood.

"For custodial neglect, unlawful deprivation, and concealment of prisoner harm, I execute punitive contract."

Corvin took one step back. "Advisor, wait -"

Sehir caught his forearm and spoke terms.

White script flashed beneath Corvin's sleeve and sank into skin as a narrow ring hidden under the cuff.

Corvin gripped the table, breath sharp.

"You will maintain lawful custody records, cease unauthorized harm, preserve confidentiality on my directives, and support transfer operations without sabotage," Sehir said. "Breach triggers immediate correction."

Corvin looked at him, pale with fury and suddenly very controlled.

"You bound me."

"I sentenced you," Sehir said.

When Corvin left, his steps were steady. He did not look back.

By morning, isolation rosters were cleaner. Meal logs reconciled. Two guards with repeated abuse complaints were reassigned off inmate contact by Corvin's own order.

Sehir made no comment.

He simply continued.

Three days into transfer operations, Corvin brought one final slate.

No name.

Only a cell designation.

"The woman in the maximum-security vault has requested an audience with you in her cell," Corvin said.

"Take me there," Sehir answered.

They descended to the lower corridor.

At her cell, Corvin opened both bolts and stepped aside. The woman within rose with deliberate grace, dirt and torn fabric unable to hide the bearing of rank.

Corvin moved to enter.

She spoke first. "If this is to be useful, I will speak only to the executor."

Corvin frowned. "You do not set conditions here."

Sehir raised a hand. "Outside."

Corvin hesitated.

The mark beneath his cuff flashed once.

He stepped out. The door closed.

For a moment, only distant surf moved through stone.

Then the woman inclined her head. "Better."

"You warned me about Arienne," Sehir said. "Why?"

"Because I needed you alive," she replied. "And I don't disclose sensitive terms in front of unverified loyalties."

"Who are you?"

"Velrathi. Former high priestess in an infernal-aligned hierarchy. Defector before final war mobilization."

Her gaze did not leave his.

"This cell is warded to hide my soul-signature from those I betrayed. If I leave without suppression, I will be found."

"By whom?"

"My former patron. Its hierarchy. Their claimants."

She looked at his hands.

"What you used in Blackstone is judicial soul-law. Rare now. Nearly lost."

Sehir said nothing.

She continued in an even voice.

"I can help you build contract structures that survive pressure, loophole work, and infernal interference. I cannot do that from inside this ward, and I cannot do it unbound."

"You are asking for punitive binding."

"Slave contract," she said. "Explicit."

Silence sat between them.

"Terms," Sehir said.

"No surrender to infernal claimants. No transfer to capital custody without your direct order. Functional service under your authority."

"And restitution?"

"Instruction. Intelligence on surviving infernal channels. Operational support until debt and threat reach lawful balance."

Sehir measured the geometry of it.

Risk contained.

Future harm prevented.

Equivalent burden.

He stepped forward.

"State acceptance."

The woman knelt, spine straight.

"I accept punitive slave contract under your execution and enforcement."

Sehir laid his hand at her throat.

White light filled the cell, hot and silent. Tight rings of script burned through the air and then sank into her skin as a narrow silver choker mark.

She inhaled sharply as the binding settled.

The ward sigils in the walls dimmed, then stabilized at half strength.

"Name," Sehir said.

"Serathis Veyr," she answered.

She rose, and for the first time a sliver of relief cracked her composure.

"You are not asking the question you should," she said quietly.

"Which question?"

"What name the old wars gave a man whose power awakens in a collapsing mine."

Sehir's expression stayed unreadable.

"If the answer matters," he said, "it can wait until we are harder to kill."

He opened the door.

Corvin stood outside, neutral and unreadable.

"Transfer authorization approved," Sehir said. "High-risk specialist asset under sealed custody notation."

Corvin nodded once. "As ordered."

By week's end, the first contracted labor columns were moving toward Blackstone under fortress escort and court mage supervision.

Publicly, it looked routine: emergency penal labor reconstruction under lawful custody.

Privately, it was the first layer of a quiet counterstate.

Sehir intended to build quickly, discreetly, and far from the queen's immediate reach.

He would not return to the capital exposed.

When Blackstone could defend itself - walls, workforce, supply, command - he would let one story travel east: Advisor Sehir died in the collapse, body unrecovered beneath stone.

Dead men are difficult to assassinate.

From the shadows of a place the court believed buried, justice could move without warning.
