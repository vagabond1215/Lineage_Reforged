# Chapter 1

The bells of the capital tolled the hour of judgment.

From the high windows of the Hall of Petitions, the sound rolled across slate rooftops and market squares before fading into the distant harbor winds. Within the hall itself, hundreds of citizens watched in respectful silence as a tall man in modest court robes stood beside the king’s throne, one hand resting lightly upon a thick ledger of statutes.

Sehir turned a page with deliberate care.

“By the statutes of Crown and Charter,” he said calmly, his voice carrying easily through the chamber, “the dispute over the irrigation canal falls under provincial authority, not guild authority. The farmers’ claim stands.”

A murmur of approval moved through the gathered crowd. Farmers in rough wool nodded in relief while the guild representatives exchanged resigned looks. Few argued with Sehir when he spoke in matters of law. Over two decades of service had made his judgments as steady and reliable as the kingdom’s own bedrock. The king leaned toward him with a faint smile.

“You make it sound so simple,” the monarch said quietly.

Sehir allowed himself the hint of a smile. “Most problems are simple, Your Majesty. It is people who make them complicated.” The king chuckled softly, and the court moved on to its next petition.

From a balcony above the hall, unseen by most of the gathering below, the queen watched with thoughtful eyes. She was elegant as ever—poised, dignified, the perfect image of royal grace. The citizens loved her for it. They loved her speeches about roads and ports and trade routes. They loved the prosperity that seemed to follow every one of her initiatives.

What they did not see were the ledgers hidden beneath those initiatives. Nor the quiet messages passed through trusted agents. Nor the careful redirection of gold, influence, and power that flowed wherever she wished it. And lately, far too many of those flows had begun to intersect with one man.

Sehir.

Her gaze lingered on him as he spoke with the king again, explaining another matter with patient clarity. To the world he was a scholar of law, a nobleman trained only enough in swordplay to satisfy tradition. A man of books and statutes. A harmless man.

Yet his investigations into provincial corruption had begun uncovering threads she had spent years weaving into perfect secrecy. He did not yet know the pattern. But he was close enough to frighten her. The queen turned away from the balcony rail. The solution had already been arranged.

Several weeks earlier, a proposal had appeared before the royal council: an inspection of a long-abandoned mining camp hidden deep within a remote valley between mountain ranges. The mine had once produced iron of excellent quality before collapsing tunnels and monster infestations drove workers away decades ago.

Restoring it could bring prosperity to the region. Or so the proposal claimed. In truth, the place had been chosen for its isolation. There were only two ways to reach the valley.

The faster route crossed a narrow mountain pass that thawed for only a few months each year, a treacherous five-day journey unsuitable for wagons or large caravans. Pack animals struggled there, and travelers carried most supplies on their own backs.

The safer route came by sea. Ships could dock at a lonely coastal fortress where the valley met the ocean, though reefs and cliffs allowed passage only during calm waters. From there it was another week’s march inland to the mine itself. Both routes ended in the same place.

A forgotten scar in the mountains where accidents were common, monsters plentiful, and witnesses nonexistent.

Perfect.

The mission itself appeared harmless enough: Sehir would accompany two scholars to survey the mine and determine whether it could be restored. One specialized in ancient engineering and mining techniques.

The other was a rising academic talent—a brilliant lightning mage whose gentle demeanor and charitable work had earned her an admirable reputation. Bright. Kind. Charismatic.

Deadly.

Far beneath her scholarly robes, she carried the training of the queen’s most capable operatives: twin blades balanced for close combat and lightning magic refined not for spectacle, but for killing.

By the time the expedition reached the mine, the valley would provide many convenient explanations for tragedy.

A cave-in.

A monster attack.

Perhaps both.

The queen allowed herself the smallest smile. Soon, Sehir would be gone.

—

Far beyond the palace walls, in a quiet shrine forgotten by most of the world, a single candle flickered before the weathered statue of a blindfolded woman holding a broken scale. The shrine had once been grand.

Now dust gathered along the cracked marble floor and weeds grew between the stones of its courtyard. Yet the candle continued to burn. Within the fading presence of the statue, something stirred.

A goddess who had once been worshiped across kingdoms now felt her power reduced to little more than a whisper. Justice had grown quiet in the world. Belief had thinned. Only one thread still connected her fully to the mortal realm. A man who had long ago set aside the armor of a forgotten order.

A man who no longer carried the weapon that had once earned him a name spoken with awe and terror across battlefields.

Cataclysm.

The goddess watched him from afar as he closed the great ledger in the royal court and bowed respectfully to the king. He did not remember the weight of the armor he once wore. He did not remember the divine fire he once wielded. But she remembered.

And soon, in the depths of a forgotten mine where stone itself would rise against him, the world would remember as well. Morning light filtered through tall glass windows of pale blue and gold as Sehir was escorted into the queen’s solar.

Unlike the crowded halls of court, the chamber was quiet and warm, scented faintly with lavender oil and polished cedar. Maps of the kingdom lay unfurled across a broad oak table near the windows, their inked roads and rivers held flat beneath brass weights shaped like lions. The queen stood beside the table, studying one of the maps with careful attention.

She did not immediately turn when Sehir entered.

“Your Majesty,” Sehir said, bowing with practiced respect.

Only then did she glance up, her expression brightening with that familiar warmth the people of the kingdom so adored.

“Advisor Sehir. Thank you for coming so quickly.”

“It is my duty.”

The queen gestured to the table. “Come, please. I wished to speak with you about a matter that requires… a particular kind of mind.” Sehir stepped forward, eyes moving instinctively to the map she had been studying. The western coast of the kingdom.

A long valley cut between two mountain chains ran from the ocean deep into the interior. At the western end sat a small fortress drawn upon the cliffs where the valley met the sea. Far inland, near the eastern mountains, a faded mark labeled an old mining settlement. He recognized the place at once.

“Blackstone Camp,” Sehir said quietly. “Abandoned nearly forty years ago after the tunnels collapsed.”

The queen nodded.

“Indeed. And yet the ore deposits were never exhausted. Some within the treasury believe the mine could still prove valuable if restored.”

She traced a finger along the valley road.

“Before committing such an undertaking, however, we require a proper assessment.”

Sehir studied the map silently.

“I assume the treasury has engineers capable of such an evaluation.”

“They do,” the queen said smoothly. “And they will accompany the expedition.”

She folded her hands behind her back.

“But the matter is not solely one of engineering.”

Sehir raised an eyebrow slightly.

“The valley is isolated,” she continued. “The roads are poor, the forests dense with beasts, and the mines themselves have long been unmaintained. Any restoration would require securing the region, fortifying the camp, and ensuring safe movement of goods through the valley.”

Her gaze lifted to meet his.

“You, Advisor Sehir, possess something few others in the court do.”

He waited.

“Perspective,” she said. “You understand law, governance, and logistics. You also understand how people behave when placed under pressure. And despite your modesty, I am aware that your noble upbringing included some degree of martial training.”

The faintest hint of amusement crossed Sehir’s face.

“Enough to survive a fencing lesson, Your Majesty. Hardly the expertise of a soldier.”

“Perhaps not,” she replied lightly, “but you possess something more valuable than swordsmanship.”

She tapped the map again.

“Tactical judgment.”

Sehir considered her words carefully.

“You wish me to determine whether the mine can be defended and governed effectively if reopened.”

“Precisely.”

He glanced again toward the mark representing the abandoned settlement.

“And the journey?”

“We will travel by ship,” she said. “The coastal fortress at the mouth of the valley will serve as your landing point.”

Her finger moved westward to the small fortress icon.

“You know the place?”

“I do.”

“It also houses the kingdom’s western prison complex,” she added. “A matter I thought might interest our senior advisor on justice.”

Sehir’s attention sharpened slightly.

“The fortress has not been inspected in some time.”

“So I am told,” the queen said pleasantly. “Your presence would allow a proper review of its conditions and administration while you pass through.”

A practical arrangement. Two purposes served with one journey. Still, Sehir remained thoughtful.

“You intend to send scholars,” he said.

“Yes.”

“One of them is an engineer?”

“Professor Aldren,” she confirmed. “An expert in ancient mining structures.”

“And the second?”

The queen’s smile returned.

“A promising young mage from the academy. Lady Arienne Valis. She has studied elemental energy and geological stability. Her talents should prove useful when examining the tunnels.”

“And,” the queen added with careful casualness, “she will also serve as your escort.”

Sehir blinked once.

“My escort?”

“She is more than capable of defending herself—and others,” the queen said reassuringly. “Lightning magic, as I understand it. Quite formidable when necessary.”

He considered this.

“So the expedition consists of three.”

“Three scholars,” she corrected gently.

Sehir allowed himself a small chuckle.

“I suspect Professor Aldren would protest that description if he heard it.”

“Perhaps,” she admitted.

The queen stepped away from the table and walked toward the windows, gazing out over the palace gardens below.

“I would not send you if I believed the journey carried grave danger,” she said. “The valley lies well within our borders. You may encounter wild beasts, perhaps the occasional wandering monster from the forests, but nothing beyond what a capable mage and a sensible advisor could manage.”

Her tone was calm, reasonable.

Reassuring.

“Mother Nature herself is often the greatest obstacle in such places,” she added. “Storms, difficult terrain, the usual inconveniences of travel.”

Sehir looked again at the map. Ship to the fortress. Inspection of the prison. A week’s journey inland to the mine. A survey of its potential. All perfectly sensible. After a moment, he nodded.

“When would you have us depart?”

The queen’s answer came without hesitation.

“The next favorable tide.”

Sehir bowed slightly.

“Then I will begin preparations.”

“Excellent,” she said warmly.

As he turned to leave, the queen watched him with calm, approving eyes. Only when the doors of the solar closed behind him did her expression change. The warmth faded. Her gaze drifted back to the map. To the long valley. To the forgotten mine at its eastern end.

“Safe travels, Advisor,” she murmured softly to the empty room.

Far from the capital, the mountains waited in silence.
