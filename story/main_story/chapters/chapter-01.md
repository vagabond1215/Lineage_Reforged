# Chapter 1

The bells of the capital tolled the hour of judgment.

From the high windows of the Hall of Petitions, the sound rolled across slate rooftops and crowded market lanes before dissolving into the distant harbor wind. Inside the hall, hundreds of citizens stood in disciplined silence as a tall man in modest court robes rested one hand on a heavy ledger beside the king's throne.

Sehir turned a page with deliberate care.

"By Crown and Charter," he said, his voice carrying cleanly across the chamber, "the irrigation dispute falls under provincial authority, not guild authority. The farmers' claim stands."

A murmur of relief moved through the crowd. Farmers in rough wool exchanged grateful looks while the guild delegation accepted the ruling with tight resignation. Few people argued when Sehir spoke on matters of law. Two decades in service had made his judgment feel as steady as the kingdom's bedrock.

The king leaned toward him with a faint smile.

"You always make it sound simple."

Sehir allowed himself a small one in return. "Most problems are simple, Your Majesty. People are what make them complicated."

The king chuckled, and the next petition was called.

From a balcony above, unseen by most in the hall below, the queen watched with patient, thoughtful eyes.

She looked as she always looked in public: poised, gracious, elegant, the image of royal composure. Citizens adored her for it. They adored her speeches about roads, ports, and trade. They adored the prosperity that seemed to follow each initiative she championed.

They did not see the ledgers behind those initiatives.

They did not see the private correspondences, the redirected contracts, the quiet transfer of coin and influence toward channels only she controlled.

And lately, too many of those channels had begun crossing paths with one man.

Sehir.

Her gaze lingered on him as he spoke with the king again, calm and precise as ever. To the world he was a legal scholar and a nobleman with enough martial training to satisfy tradition.

A harmless man.

Yet his corruption inquiries had started to catch threads she had spent years weaving into invisibility.

He still did not see the full pattern.

But he was close enough to be dangerous.

The queen turned from the balcony rail.

The solution had already been arranged.

Weeks earlier, a proposal had reached the royal council: dispatch an inspection team to a long-abandoned mining settlement deep in a western mountain valley. The mine had once produced high-quality iron before tunnel collapse and monster activity drove workers out decades ago.

Restoring it, the proposal argued, could return prosperity to the region.

Perhaps.

But the site had been chosen for a different reason: isolation.

There were only two ways to reach the valley.

The faster route crossed a narrow mountain pass that thawed for only part of the year, a five-day climb too dangerous for proper caravans and unreliable for pack trains.

The safer route came by sea. Ships could land at a coastal fortress where the valley opened into the ocean, though reefs and cliff channels allowed passage only in calm waters. From there it was another week's march inland to the mine.

Either way led to the same destination.

A forgotten wound in the mountains where accidents were common, monsters plentiful, and witnesses scarce.

Perfect.

The mission itself looked harmless. Sehir would travel with two scholars to assess whether the mine could be restored and secured. One was an authority on ancient mining and structural engineering.

The other was a rising academic star - brilliant, charitable, and publicly beloved. A lightning mage with a reputation for kindness.

Bright.

Graceful.

Deadly.

Under her scholar's cloak she carried twin curved blades and the training of one of the queen's most effective covert operatives. By the time the team reached the mine, the valley would offer all the explanations anyone needed.

A cave-in.

A monster attack.

Both, if useful.

The queen allowed herself the smallest smile.

Soon, Sehir would be gone.

-

Far beyond the palace walls, in a shrine forgotten by nearly everyone, a single candle burned before a weathered statue of a blindfolded woman holding a broken scale.

Once, the shrine had been grand.

Now dust gathered on cracked marble, and weeds grew between courtyard stones.

Still, the candle endured.

Within the fading presence bound to the statue, something stirred.

A goddess who had once been worshiped across kingdoms had dwindled to a whisper. Justice had thinned in the world. Belief had thinned with it.

One thread still connected her fully to mortal life.

A man who had long ago set down the armor of a forgotten order.

A man who no longer carried the weapon that had once made armies speak his name in fear.

Cataclysm.

The goddess watched him from afar as he closed the court ledger and bowed beside the king.

He did not remember the weight of that armor.

He did not remember the fire he had once commanded.

She remembered both.

And soon, in a buried mine where stone itself would rise against him, the world would remember as well.

Morning light poured through blue-and-gold glass when Sehir was escorted into the queen's solar.

Unlike the crowded court, the chamber was quiet and warm, faintly scented with lavender and polished cedar. Maps lay spread across an oak table near the windows, held flat by brass lion-weights.

The queen stood over one map with careful attention and did not look up immediately as he entered.

"Your Majesty," Sehir said, bowing.

Only then did she turn, wearing that familiar warmth the public loved so much.

"Advisor Sehir. Thank you for coming so quickly."

"It is my duty."

She gestured him closer. "I wanted your counsel on a matter requiring a particular kind of mind."

Sehir stepped to the table and followed her map line toward the western coast. A long valley cut between mountain chains. A fortress at the mouth. A faded mark far inland where the old settlement sat.

He recognized it at once.

"Blackstone Camp," he said quietly. "Abandoned nearly forty years ago after tunnel failure."

The queen nodded.

"And yet the ore field was never exhausted. Some in the treasury believe the mine could be reclaimed."

Her finger traced the valley road.

"Before we commit, we need a proper assessment."

Sehir studied the map in silence. "The treasury has engineers for that."

"It does," she said smoothly. "And they will be on the expedition."

She folded her hands behind her back.

"But engineering is only part of the question."

Sehir lifted an eyebrow.

"The valley is isolated. Roads are poor. Forest routes are beast-heavy. The mine itself is unstable. Reopening it means governance, security, logistics, and discipline under stress."

She met his gaze.

"You have perspective few at court possess."

He waited.

"You understand law, administration, and supply movement. You understand how people behave under pressure. And despite your modesty, I know your upbringing included at least some martial training."

A faint smile touched Sehir's mouth.

"Enough to survive a fencing lesson. Hardly military expertise."

"Perhaps," she said lightly. "But tactical judgment is worth more than showy swordwork."

He considered her words.

"You want me to assess whether Blackstone can be defended and governed if reopened."

"Precisely."

He glanced again at the map mark.

"And travel?"

"By ship," she said. "You'll land at the coastal fortress."

Her finger tapped the western icon.

"You know it?"

"I do."

"Then this will interest you. The kingdom's western prison complex is housed there as well. Your visit would allow a full inspection while in transit."

Sehir's attention sharpened.

"That facility has not been formally reviewed in some time."

"So I am told," she said with practiced ease.

A practical arrangement.

Two objectives, one journey.

Still, Sehir remained thoughtful.

"You said two scholars. One is Professor Aldren?"

"Yes. Expert in ancient mine architecture."

"And the second?"

The queen's smile returned.

"Lady Arienne Valis. Academy lightning mage. Studies in elemental energy and geological stability. Her skills should prove useful in the tunnels."

She paused just long enough to make the next sentence sound incidental.

"She will also serve as your escort."

Sehir blinked once.

"My escort?"

"She is quite capable of defending herself and others," the queen said. "Lightning magic can be persuasive when required."

He weighed that.

"So the expedition is three scholars."

"Three scholars," she echoed gently.

Sehir gave a brief laugh. "Aldren may dispute the label."

"He may," she said.

She moved to the windows and looked out over the palace gardens.

"I would not send you if I believed this mission gravely dangerous. The valley is within our borders. You may meet wild beasts or wandering monsters, but nothing beyond what a capable mage and sensible advisor can manage."

Her tone was calm, reassuring.

"Nature itself will likely be the greater obstacle. Storms, terrain, delays - the usual inconveniences."

Sehir studied the map again.

Ship to Westfall.

Prison inspection.

Week inland.

Mine survey.

All of it looked reasonable on paper.

At last he nodded.

"When do we depart?"

"At the next favorable tide," she answered without hesitation.

Sehir bowed. "Then I will begin preparations."

"Excellent," she said warmly.

He turned to leave. The queen watched him with calm approval until the solar doors closed behind him.

Then the warmth left her face.

Her gaze dropped back to the map.

The valley.

The mine.

"Safe travels, Advisor," she murmured to the empty room.

Far from the capital, the mountains waited.
