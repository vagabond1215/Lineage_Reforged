## Use Rules

- Historical records describe a place, institution, household, workforce, voyage, or provisioning system; none supplies a universal medieval diet or calorie target.
- `Issued`, `purchased`, `supplied`, `carried`, `prepared`, `edible`, and `consumed` are different measurements. A source is not silently moved from one category to another.
- Modern physiology and operational-ration sources are comparisons for demand variation and package form, not evidence of medieval practice or exact ration balance.
- Calorie estimates reconstructed from historical accounts inherit uncertainty in weights, edible fractions, recipes, distribution, waste, activity, and modern food-composition conversion.
- The integration artifact cites source IDs from this index. Game recommendations are inferences and are labeled as such.

## Evidence Register

### S01 — Human Energy Requirements

- **Title:** *Human Energy Requirements: Report of a Joint FAO/WHO/UNU Expert Consultation*
- **Author/institution:** Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations; World Health Organization; United Nations University
- **Publication date:** 2004; consultation held 17–24 October 2001
- **URL/stable identifier:** [FAO Food and Nutrition Technical Report Series 1](https://www.fao.org/4/y5686e/y5686e00.htm)
- **Source type:** International expert-consultation report; modern physiology comparison
- **Exact claim supported:** Human energy requirement is energy intake that balances expenditure while supporting health, body size/composition, and necessary activity; requirements vary with body size, age/life stage, sex as mediated through body composition and activity, and physical activity level.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Modern human physiology; international methodological guidance
- **Limitations:** Population and group estimation does not predict a specific individual. It is not evidence of medieval intake, food access, or ration issue.
- **Audit use:** Supports a character-specific daily energy target and rejects one universal historical calorie number.

### S02 — Energy Requirements Of Adults

- **Title:** *Energy Requirements of Adults*
- **Author/institution:** FAO/WHO/UNU expert consultation
- **Publication date:** 2004
- **URL/stable identifier:** [Chapter 5 of Human Energy Requirements](https://www.fao.org/4/y5686e/y5686e07.htm)
- **Source type:** International technical report chapter; modern physiology comparison
- **Exact claim supported:** Adult energy needs depend strongly on body weight and habitual physical activity. Worked tables show materially different daily totals across sedentary/light, active, and vigorous activity patterns rather than one fixed allowance.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Modern adults; illustrative reference populations
- **Limitations:** Worked examples are not game balance values and do not encode climate, illness, food availability, or historical labor conditions by themselves.
- **Audit use:** Supports activity-sensitive demand for agriculture, construction, military work, travel, hunting, gathering, craft, and sedentary periods.

### S03 — Food, Energy And The Creation Of Industriousness

- **Title:** *Food, Energy and the Creation of Industriousness: Work and Material Culture in Agrarian England, 1550–1780*
- **Author/institution:** Craig Muldrew; Cambridge University Press
- **Publication date:** 2011
- **URL/stable identifier:** [Chapter record: Calories Consumed by Labourers](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/CBO9780511933905A012/type/BOOK_PART), DOI `10.1017/CBO9780511933905.005`
- **Source type:** Academic monograph and reconstructed historical diet analysis
- **Exact claim supported:** Household, institutional, and labor-account evidence can be used to reconstruct food-energy availability for English laborers, and strenuous work could be associated with high reconstructed energy availability.
- **Geographic/period scope:** England, principally mid-sixteenth through late-eighteenth century; laboring households and institutions
- **Limitations:** Early modern rather than broadly medieval; reconstructed availability is not necessarily individual consumption; household distribution, waste, recipe assumptions, and modern calorie conversion add uncertainty.
- **Audit use:** Comparison for heavy labor and for the issued/available-versus-consumed distinction; not a universal target.

### S04 — Food And Power In Early Medieval England: Rethinking Feorm

- **Title:** *Food and Power in Early Medieval England: Rethinking Feorm*
- **Author/institution:** Thomas Lambert and Sam Leggett; *Anglo-Saxon England*, Cambridge University Press
- **Publication date:** 2022
- **URL/stable identifier:** [Cambridge Core article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/anglo-saxon-england/article/food-and-power-in-early-medieval-england-rethinking-feorm/92CCDA9706D8F0858B0DE4CB4D51FB72), DOI `10.1017/S0263675122000082`
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed historical and quantitative analysis
- **Exact claim supported:** Early medieval English food lists often represent large feast provisions rather than routine individual daily rations; the authors' reconstructed full-loaf allocations can be very large and include substantial ale and animal products.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Early medieval England; food renders and feast-provision lists
- **Limitations:** The article itself warns that these are best read as feast or supply contexts. Reconstructed calories are not evidence that one person routinely consumed the entire allocation.
- **Audit use:** Strong warning against treating an institutional food list as ordinary daily intake; supports separate feast/group-provision semantics.

### S05 — Consumption Of Field Crops In Late Medieval England

- **Title:** *The Consumption of Field Crops in Late Medieval England*
- **Author/institution:** Christopher Dyer; in *Food in Medieval England: Diet and Nutrition*, Oxford University Press
- **Publication date:** 2006
- **URL/stable identifier:** [Oxford Academic chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/47620/chapter-abstract/422427248)
- **Source type:** Academic book chapter; historical synthesis
- **Exact claim supported:** Grain was consumed through bread, ale, and pottage, with material differences by wealth and institution; large household or monastic ale totals are aggregate provisioning evidence.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Late medieval England
- **Limitations:** England is not all medieval Europe. Household purchases and institutional allowances do not establish equal individual consumption, and liquid measures/strength vary.
- **Audit use:** Supports diverse meal forms, socioeconomic variation, and the warning that aggregate stores or allowances are not individual intake.

### S06 — The Medieval Household At Goodrich Castle

- **Title:** *The Medieval Household at Goodrich Castle*
- **Author/institution:** English Heritage
- **Publication date:** Web feature; publication date not stated
- **URL/stable identifier:** [English Heritage](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/medievalhousehold)
- **Source type:** High-quality public-history synthesis grounded in household accounts
- **Exact claim supported:** Joan de Valence's 1296 household accounts document large-scale acquisition and service for an elite household, including provisions supporting many people and two principal meals.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Goodrich Castle, England, 1296; elite household
- **Limitations:** One noble household and accounting episode; purchases include staff/guests and status-based allocation, and do not establish per-person consumption.
- **Audit use:** Household/group provisioning, meal-service scale, unequal distribution, and elite-house safety/resources context.

### S07 — Ship's Biscuit

- **Title:** *Ship's Biscuit*
- **Author/institution:** Royal Museums Greenwich
- **Publication date:** Web feature; publication date not stated
- **URL/stable identifier:** [Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/maritime-history/ships-biscuit)
- **Source type:** Museum public-history synthesis
- **Exact claim supported:** Ship's biscuit was a durable maritime staple before canned foods; a cited 1588 theoretical daily allowance included one pound of biscuit, while later naval administration standardized broader rations.
- **Geographic/period scope:** English/British maritime context, late sixteenth century and later
- **Limitations:** Early modern maritime issue, not medieval household practice; an allowance is not proof of amount eaten, and voyages, substitutions, infestation, spoilage, and distribution matter.
- **Audit use:** Carried/issued durable provisions comparison and maritime-specific limitations.

### S08 — Pre-Industrial Salterns

- **Title:** *Pre-Industrial Salterns: Introductions to Heritage Assets*
- **Author/institution:** Historic England
- **Publication date:** 2018
- **URL/stable identifier:** [Historic England guidance PDF](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/publications/iha-preindustrial-salterns/heag225-pre-industrial-salterns/)
- **Source type:** Government heritage synthesis
- **Exact claim supported:** Before refrigeration and canning, salting, drying, and smoking were major preservation practices supporting storage, seasonal resilience, shipment, and trade.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Britain; pre-industrial salt production and use over a long period
- **Limitations:** Broad heritage context, not a recipe, safety guarantee, or quantitative shelf-life source. Preservation methods overlap and outcomes depend on process quality and storage.
- **Audit use:** Historical preservation context and support for independent process-method and preservation-effect axes.

### S09 — Meal, Ready-To-Eat

- **Title:** *Meal, Ready-To-Eat (MRE)*
- **Author/institution:** Defense Logistics Agency Troop Support, United States Department of Defense
- **Publication date:** Current operational-ration page; accessed 2026-07-20
- **URL/stable identifier:** [DLA operational-ration specification page](https://www.dla.mil/Troop-Support/Subsistence/Operational-rations/MRE/)
- **Source type:** Government operational-ration specification; modern comparison
- **Exact claim supported:** One modern MRE is a self-contained meal for one person engaged in heavy activity and averages about 1,250 kcal; it contains multiple components and may be eaten without ordinary food-service facilities.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Contemporary United States military operational feeding
- **Limitations:** Modern formulation, packaging, fortification, logistics, body assumptions, and food safety are not medieval. Issued energy still need not equal consumed energy.
- **Audit use:** Comparison showing that one modern operational meal can contain multiple components; no transfer of naming, calories, packaging technology, or ration bands.

### S10 — Food Balance Sheets: A Handbook

- **Title:** *Food Balance Sheets: A Handbook*
- **Author/institution:** Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations
- **Publication date:** 2001
- **URL/stable identifier:** [FAO handbook, Nature of Food Balance Sheets](https://www.fao.org/4/X9892E/X9892e01.htm)
- **Source type:** International statistical-method handbook
- **Exact claim supported:** Food-balance sheets estimate supply available for human consumption after specified uses/losses; they do not show actual intake by individuals or distribution among population groups, and loss/waste/stock data may be incomplete.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Modern national statistical systems; general methodology
- **Limitations:** Not a historical diet source and not an individual dietary survey.
- **Audit use:** Formal vocabulary for supply, availability, waste, and actual consumption; supports the audit's measurement ladder.

### S11 — Not Eating Enough: Overcoming Underconsumption Of Military Operational Rations

- **Title:** *Not Eating Enough: Overcoming Underconsumption of Military Operational Rations*
- **Author/institution:** Institute of Medicine Committee on Military Nutrition; National Academies Press
- **Publication date:** 1995
- **URL/stable identifier:** [NCBI Bookshelf edition](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK232462/), National Academies DOI `10.17226/5002`
- **Source type:** Expert committee report; modern operational-ration research
- **Exact claim supported:** Food provided in operational rations can exceed food actually consumed; acceptability, variety/boredom, time, context, and access to hot meals affect intake.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Modern military exercises and operational feeding studies
- **Limitations:** Modern soldiers, foods, packaging, and study conditions; does not establish medieval morale mechanics or a universal penalty curve.
- **Audit use:** Supports separate issued/carried/consumed measures and a bounded, difficulty-adjustable food-fatigue system.

### S12 — Effect Of Food Variety On Intake Of A Meal

- **Title:** *Effect of Food Variety on Intake of a Meal: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis*
- **Author/institution:** Rochelle Embling et al.; *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*
- **Publication date:** 2021
- **URL/stable identifier:** [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33515033/), DOI `10.1093/ajcn/nqaa352`
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed systematic review and meta-analysis; modern physiology/behavior comparison
- **Exact claim supported:** Across experimental human studies, greater within- or across-meal variety was associated with greater intake, but heterogeneity and risk of bias were substantial.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Modern experimental human studies published from 1980 through 2019
- **Limitations:** Does not prove a medieval morale bonus, exact decay period, or that every tiny ingredient meaningfully changes intake.
- **Audit use:** Supports dose/dominance/recency-aware diversity rather than a binary ingredient-count bonus.

### S14 — Safe Food Handling

- **Title:** *Safe Food Handling*
- **Author/institution:** United States Food and Drug Administration
- **Publication date:** Current guidance page; accessed 2026-07-20
- **URL/stable identifier:** [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/safe-food-handling)
- **Source type:** Government food-safety guidance; modern comparison
- **Exact claim supported:** Safe handling separates cleaning, raw/cooked separation, cooking, and chilling; different foods have different safe-temperature guidance, and perishable food risk depends on time and temperature.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Contemporary United States consumer guidance
- **Limitations:** Refrigeration, thermometers, regulated supply chains, and exact temperature thresholds are modern and must not appear as general medieval character knowledge.

### S15 — Hot Smoking Of Fish

- **Title:** *Torry Advisory Note No. 82 (Revised): Hot Smoking of Fish*
- **Author/institution:** A. McK. Bannerman; Torry Research Station; reproduced by FAO
- **Publication date:** Revised advisory note; date not stated on the accessed web reproduction
- **URL/stable identifier:** [FAO reproduction](https://www.fao.org/4/x5953e/x5953e00.htm)
- **Source type:** Government technical advisory note; modern processing/safety comparison
- **Exact claim supported:** Hot smoking can make some fish ready to eat while also drying/preserving, but safety depends on raw material, brining, smoking/drying, cooling, packing, storage, and relevant salt concentration; smoking is not a single universal effect.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Modern commercial fish processing in the United Kingdom context
- **Limitations:** Modern controlled process, not medieval practice or a fantasy-species safety rule. It does not show that cold smoking, arbitrary smoking, or all fish become safe.
- **Audit use:** Key evidence for overlapping cooking/preservation methods and residual hazard.

### S16 — From The Bakehouse To The Courthouse

- **Title:** *From the Bakehouse to the Courthouse: Bakers, Baking, and the Assize of Bread in Late Medieval England*
- **Author/institution:** Sarah Peters Kernan; *Food & History*, Brepols
- **Publication date:** 2014
- **URL/stable identifier:** [Brepols article](https://www.brepolsonline.net/doi/abs/10.1484/J.FOOD.5.108966), DOI `10.1484/J.FOOD.5.108966`
- **Source type:** Peer-reviewed historical article
- **Exact claim supported:** Late medieval towns regulated bread weight and quality and court records document violations; price, weight, product grade, inspection, and enforcement were distinct concerns.
- **Geographic/period scope:** Oxford 1309–1338, Southampton 1482–1483, and London 1292–1437
- **Limitations:** Bread-market regulation in selected English towns does not establish all food fraud, all regions, universal enforcement, or modern scientific inspection.
- **Audit use:** Historical support for short weight, substitution/quality claims, inspection institutions, and contextual rather than omniscient trust.

## Comparison-Only Modern Formats

The following are design analogies, not historical or nutritional calibration sources:

- **Protein bar or granola pack:** compact single component or supplement; should not automatically mean one complete meal.
- **Bento or packed lunch:** one-person composed meal with multiple visible portions; useful UI/manifest analogy only.
- **Picnic basket:** shared meal/provisions carried in a container; not a personal ration solely because it is portable.
- **Modern MRE:** one-person operational meal with multiple components; only S09's scoped modern specification may be cited.

No modern format authorizes plastic packaging, fortification, Earth product names, exact calories, or modern safety knowledge as repository content.

## User-Authored Game-Balance Posture

The following approximations are accepted user-authored game-design direction. They are not findings from S01–S17 and must not be cited as universal medieval intake:

- approximately `2,500 kcal/day` for an ordinary healthy active adult;
- approximately `3,500-4,000 kcal/day` for sustained high-intensity labor, loaded marching, armored military activity, heavy farming, threshing, construction, or comparable exertion.

Future demand may vary with activity, body, climate, injury, illness, age/life stage, load, terrain, pace, combat, training, and recovery under accepted owners. The observed live `dailyCalories: 100` rule remains a legacy game-scale body-state value; it does not convert these approximations into percentages or authorize a 100-point ration-energy model. Exact ration kilocalories, manifests, sizes, serving potential, and balance remain open.

## Source-Quality Conclusion

The evidence is sufficient for bounded game-design recommendations and owner separation. It is not sufficient to finalize calorie balance, portion mass/volume, spoilage rates, universal medieval menus, or fraud frequency. Those remain authored and balance decisions, not historical facts.
